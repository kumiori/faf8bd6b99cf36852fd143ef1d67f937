
I. CONCEPTUAL FOUNDATION

1. The Commons Game

From collective intelligence to collective action.

The Commons Game is a multiplayer, state-changing experiment in collective organization.

Players inhabit an evolving ecosystem of:

* Actors and organizations
* Relationships
* Capacities and expertise
* Needs
* Resources
* Agreements
* Commitments
* Governance arrangements
* Collective objectives
* Evidence and verified outcomes

Players can discover, connect, negotiate, assemble coalitions, establish commitments, and attempt collective actions.

The objective is not to maximize connectivity.

The objective is to transform the collective into a configuration capable of accomplishing something that was previously impossible.

Fundamental question

What makes a collection of individuals capable of acting together?

The engine must support experiments addressing this question without imposing a predetermined answer.

⸻

II. THE FUNDAMENTAL DISTINCTION

2. Commitment versus Commit

This distinction is frozen and must be reflected explicitly in the architecture.

2.1 Commitment

A commitment is a social, institutional, or contractual undertaking.

It represents a promise, agreement, or accepted responsibility.

It may involve:

* One or several actors
* An object or objective
* Conditions
* Responsibilities
* Resources
* Deadlines
* Authorization requirements
* Verification criteria

Commitments emphasize:

Trust, reciprocity, responsibility, and cooperation.

A commitment does not automatically guarantee fulfillment.

2.2 Commit

A commit is an authorized and validated modification of the authoritative collective state.

Formally:

[
S_{t+1}=T(S_t,a_t)
]

where the transition is accepted only when its preconditions and authorization requirements are satisfied.

A commit must have:

* A unique identifier
* A reference to its initiating action
* An identifiable author or initiating actor
* An authorization record
* A previous state or version reference
* A resulting state or version reference
* A timestamp
* An auditable event record

A commit is not synonymous with successful real-world execution.

For example:

* Accepting an agreement can produce a commit.
* Reserving a resource can produce a commit.
* Establishing a governance rule can produce a commit.
* Recording evidence of fulfillment can produce a commit.

These are different kinds of state transitions.

2.3 The central invariant

[
\boxed{
\text{Accepted commitment}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{Verified fulfillment}
}
]

The engine must never silently collapse intentions, promises, authorizations, actions, and verified outcomes.

⸻

III. THE COLLECTIVE STATE

3. State representation

The initial conceptual representation is:

[
\boxed{
S_t=(V_t,E_t,C_t,N_t,R_t,K_t)
}
]

where:

* (V_t): actors
* (E_t): relationships
* (C_t): capacities
* (N_t): needs
* (R_t): resources
* (K_t): commitments

This is a conceptual state decomposition, not a mandatory Python data structure.

The implementation must also accommodate:

* Agreements
* Governance rules
* Permissions and authorizations
* Scenarios and objectives
* Evidence
* Event history

Do not overload the state tuple with unrelated implementation details.

Prefer a typed, extensible domain model.

3.1 State versus knowledge

Distinguish:

1. The simulated collective state.
2. The observed or documented state.
3. The declared state.
4. Hypothetical or inferred possibilities.

For example, a relationship may be declared but unverified.

A capacity may be reported without being demonstrated.

A potential collaboration may be inferred without being accepted.

Every relevant assertion should preserve its epistemic status and provenance.

Discovery of information is not necessarily a modification of the underlying collective.

The engine must support both changes in knowledge and changes in collective organization.

⸻

IV. THE MULTIPLEX ECOSYSTEM

4. Network structure

Represent the collective as an evolving multiplex network:

[
\mathcal G_t=(V_t,E_t^{(1)},\ldots,E_t^{(L)})
]

Different layers may represent:

* Documented relationships
* Declared affiliations
* Shared domains
* Potential complementarities
* Proposed collaborations
* Accepted commitments
* Operational partnerships
* Verified exchanges

Preserve the distinction between observed and derived relationships.

4.1 From edges to agreement objects

An agreement is not merely an edge with an increased weight.

An agreement is a first-class domain object with its own lifecycle.

For a bilateral agreement:

[
A\longrightarrow K\longleftarrow B
]

where (K) represents the agreement or commitment object.

This representation must generalize to agreements involving multiple actors.

The engine should support both:

* Graph projections for visualization and analysis.
* Explicit agreement objects for governance and state transitions.

The graph is a representation of the collective, not necessarily the authoritative storage format.

⸻

V. AGREEMENTS AND CONTRACTS

5. The agreement primitive

Introduce a general agreement model.

A minimal agreement contains:

Field   Meaning
ID  Stable agreement identifier
Parties Participating actors
Object  Subject of the agreement
Terms   Responsibilities and conditions
Resources   Resources involved, if any
Validity    Temporal conditions
Authorization   Required approvals
Verification    Fulfillment criteria
Status  Current lifecycle state
Evidence    Supporting records

An agreement may be bilateral or multilateral.

A contract is a particular form of agreement, potentially carrying legal obligations.

Do not assume that every agreement is legally enforceable.

5.1 Agreement lifecycle

Support a lifecycle including:

[
\text{Draft}
\rightarrow
\text{Proposed}
\rightarrow
\text{Negotiating}
\rightarrow
\text{Accepted}
\rightarrow
\text{Authorized}
\rightarrow
\text{Active}
]

Possible subsequent states include:

* Fulfilled
* Expired
* Withdrawn
* Suspended
* Terminated
* Disputed

Not every agreement requires every intermediate state.

Transitions must be governed by explicit rules.

An agreement can be accepted before the required institutional authorization has been obtained.

Acceptance and authorization must remain separate.

5.2 Agreement versioning

Negotiation modifies proposed terms.

The engine must preserve:

* Agreement versions
* Proposed amendments
* Party acceptance of specific versions
* Superseded versions
* Evidence of authorization

Acceptance of version (n) must not silently authorize version (n+1).

⸻

VI. ACTIONS, TRANSITIONS AND COMMITS

6. Authoritative state transitions

The existing game engine should remain the authoritative mechanism for resolving actions.

Players submit proposed actions.

The engine determines whether the action is admissible.

A successful action produces a state transition and an auditable commit.

Conceptually:

[
(S_t,a_t,\omega_t)
\xrightarrow{\text{validation}}
(S_{t+1},e_t)
]

where:

* (a_t): submitted action
* (\omega_t): explicitly modeled uncertainty or external event
* (e_t): resulting event or commit record

Not every submitted action produces a commit.

Rejected actions should return an explicit reason.

6.1 Required properties

The transition mechanism must support:

Atomicity

A transition either completes or leaves the authoritative state unchanged.

Authorization

Only permitted actors may initiate or approve protected operations.

Consistency

State invariants must remain satisfied.

Auditability

Accepted transformations must be traceable.

Idempotency

Retrying the same action must not create duplicate effects.

Concurrency control

Simultaneous actions must not silently violate shared constraints.

Deterministic replay

Given the same initial state, ordered actions, and recorded external inputs, the engine should reconstruct the same resulting state.

These are requirements of the upgraded engine, not claims about the current implementation.

VII. DECENTRALIZED COORDINATION

7. Principles

The Commons Game borrows coordination primitives associated with decentralized systems and Web3.

However:

Decentralized governance does not require blockchain infrastructure.

Separate three questions:

1. Who may propose?
2. Who may authorize?
3. Who records and validates the resulting transition?

The first implementation may retain a centralized authoritative engine while enforcing decentralized authorization rules.

7.1 Digital signatures

Support an abstraction for signed acceptance of specific agreement versions.

Initially, a simpler authenticated approval mechanism may be used.

Do not equate a UI confirmation with a cryptographic signature.

7.2 Multisignature authorization

Support policies requiring multiple approvals.

Examples:

* All parties must accept.
* A threshold of participants must approve.
* Specific institutional representatives must authorize.
* Different stakeholder categories must consent.

Authorization policies must be explicit and inspectable.

7.3 Escrow

Introduce escrow as a future-capable resource-management primitive.

An escrow reserves or holds resources subject to release conditions.

Do not implement cryptocurrency custody or financial transfers in the initial milestone.

The initial abstraction should support simulated or internally managed resources.

7.4 Resource conservation

The engine must prevent incompatible reservations of resources it controls.

For a resource (r):

[
\boxed{
\sum_{K\in\mathcal K_t}q_{rK}\leq Q_r
}
]

where:

* (Q_r) is the available resource quantity.
* (q_{rK}) is the quantity reserved by agreement (K).

The invariant must be enforced atomically under concurrent submissions.

Externally controlled resources require a distinct verification or reconciliation mechanism.

7.5 Distributed ledgers

Do not introduce a blockchain dependency without a demonstrated requirement.

Provide architectural interfaces that could eventually support:

* External signatures
* Verifiable credentials
* Independent audit records
* Distributed authorization
* External settlement

Sensitive information must not be published to immutable public infrastructure.

VIII. THREE GAME MODES

The Commons Game contains three interconnected modes.

They share one authoritative state model.

8.1 ECOSYSTEM

Who can act together?

Players explore:

* Actors
* Relationships
* Expertise
* Needs
* Resources
* Potential complementarities

Possible actions:

* Discover
* Inspect
* Declare
* Connect
* Propose an exchange

The engine distinguishes discovery from creation.

8.2 SCENARIOS

What can we accomplish together?

Players:

* Establish objectives
* Assemble coalitions
* Negotiate responsibilities
* Allocate resources
* Establish agreements
* Accept commitments
* Attempt actions
* Verify outcomes

This mode provides the principal setting for contractual primitives.

8.3 GATHERING

How does a collective come into existence?

Players organize a gathering.

They may:

* Invite participants
* Allocate roles
* Coordinate resources
* Organize encounters
* Manage uncertainties
* Record emerging agreements
* Transform encounters into subsequent actions

Gathering is not merely an interface for the ecosystem.

It is a game mode with its own objectives, constraints, and transitions.

IX. THE FIRST EMPIRICAL WORLD

9. Montréal

The Commons Game originates from the Montréal workshop on cultural and digital commons, October 2026.

The empirical material includes:

* An ecosystem of organizations and relationships
* Questionnaire responses
* Expertise and needs matrices
* Potential complementarities
* Scenarios
* Action trajectories
* Collective texts
* Workshop organization and interactions

These materials constitute the initial world.

Preserve provenance.

Distinguish:

* Questionnaire declarations
* Interviews
* Independent documentary research
* Derived mathematical relationships
* Hypothetical scenarios
* Actual commitments
* Verified outcomes

Do not convert inferred relationships into documented collaborations.

9.1 Existing mathematical objects

The earlier analysis includes:

[
B=O\lor N
]

where (O) and (N) are the offered and requested expertise incidence matrices.

The directed complementarity matrix is:

[
\boxed{W=ON^\top}
]

with:

[
W_{ij}=\sum_k O_{ik}N_{jk}.
]

It counts declared expertise domains offered by participant (i) and requested by participant (j).

Missing responses must remain distinguishable from known zeros.

The engine should permit these derived structures to inform gameplay without treating potential complementarity as an accepted agreement.

X. FIRST PLAYABLE SCENARIO

10. Montréal → Geneva

The initial scenario concerns transforming an initiative emerging from Montréal into an actionable collective undertaking.

A possible objective is establishing an international mentoring network helping public institutions understand and govern AI and digital commons.

Players must:

1. Identify a shared objective.
2. Identify relevant actors.
3. Assemble a coalition.
4. Negotiate responsibilities.
5. Establish an agreement.
6. Authorize commitments.
7. Record a concrete action.
8. Provide evidence of progress or fulfillment.

Producing a manifesto is not sufficient to establish operational success.

The scenario should distinguish:

* A drafted collective text
* An accepted agreement
* A committed responsibility
* An initiated action
* A verified outcome

10.1 Minimal bilateral agreement example

A second, smaller scenario should exercise the agreement engine.

Situation: A researcher requests access to server logs for studying AI-agent activity.

The parties discuss:

* Research purpose
* Data-access conditions
* Duration
* Privacy requirements
* Institutional authorization
* Verification of delivery

The Montréal conversation concerning Gallica server logs is an illustrative real-world motivation.

Do not encode it as a finalized contract.

The existing oral agreement in principle does not establish that access has been institutionally authorized.

Use synthetic data and fictional actors in automated tests and public demonstrations.

The scenario should exercise:

* Proposal
* Negotiation
* Acceptance
* Authorization
* Activation
* Fulfillment
* Verification

XI. AI PARTICIPATION

11. AI as an instrument

AI may assist players in:

* Discovering relevant information
* Identifying potential complementarities
* Exploring alternative scenarios
* Challenging assumptions
* Drafting agreements
* Examining contradictions
* Suggesting possible actions

AI must not silently exercise governance authority.

AI-generated proposals must be distinguishable from human decisions.

An AI suggestion does not become an accepted commitment merely because it has been generated.

The engine must preserve the distinction between:

[
\text{Suggestion}
\neq
\text{Authorization}
\neq
\text{Commit}
]

Any delegated AI authority must be explicit, bounded, and auditable.

XII. MECHANICAL INTERPRETATION

12. An evolving collective

The network may be visualized as a deformable structure.

Nodes represent actors.

Connections represent relationships or interactions.

Changes in the network may modify its geometry.

An illustrative energy functional is:

[
\mathcal E(x,t)

\frac12\sum_{i,j}k_{ij}(t)
\left(|x_i-x_j|-\ell_{ij}\right)^2.
]

This is a visualization and modeling hypothesis.

It is not a calibrated mechanical description of social behavior.

The architecture should permit future exploration of:

* Structural sensitivity
* Network reorganization
* Stability
* Bifurcation
* Irreversible transformations
* Collective capacity

Do not embed mechanical assumptions into the core transition engine.

Mechanical visualization and analysis must remain optional modules.

XV. IMPLEMENTATION MILESTONE

15. Minimum viable upgrade

The first milestone must demonstrate:

A. Shared engine

Both game_hube and the Commons Game can use the reusable engine.

B. Actors

Two or more actors exist in an authoritative shared state.

C. Agreements

One actor proposes an agreement to another.

D. Negotiation

The agreement can be revised and versioned.

E. Authorization

The relevant parties approve the same agreement version.

F. Commit

The engine validates and records the resulting state transformation.

G. Resources

A resource reservation cannot exceed available capacity.

H. Verification

Fulfillment and verification remain distinct operations.

I. Replay

The recorded event history reconstructs the resulting state.

J. Compatibility

Existing game_hube functionality remains operational.

15.1 Explicitly excluded from this milestone

Do not implement:

* Cryptocurrency transfers
* Blockchain deployment
* Real financial escrow
* Token-based governance
* Automated legal enforcement
* Production identity verification
* Real institutional data exchange

These may become future adapters or application features.

XIX. FROZEN PRINCIPLES

The following principles define Commons Game V0.1.

1. The game investigates the emergence of collective agency.
2. The collective is represented as an evolving, partially observed system.
3. Potential relationships, intentions, commitments, and verified outcomes are distinct.
4. A commitment is a social or institutional undertaking emphasizing trust and reciprocity.
5. A commit is an authorized, validated, auditable transformation of the authoritative collective state.
6. Agreements are first-class objects with explicit parties, terms, authorization, and lifecycle.
7. Collective governance is represented through explicit decision and authorization rules.
8. Decentralized authority does not require decentralized infrastructure.
9. Resources and obligations must respect enforceable state invariants.
10. AI assists exploration but does not silently govern the collective.
11. The authoritative engine is independent of visualization, persistence, and application-specific interfaces.
12. The existing game_hube implementation is the starting point, not disposable scaffolding.
13. The Commons Game must remain grounded in empirical material while distinguishing evidence from inference and simulation.
14. Mechanical analogies are exploratory models, not established laws of collective behavior.
15. The objective is not simply to represent cooperation, but to make its conditions, transformations, and consequences experimentally accessible.

⸻

FINAL DIRECTIVE

Do not build a new game engine from scratch.

Inspect the existing implementation, preserve its strengths, and extend its conceptual and technical foundations.

The immediate milestone is deliberately modest:

Two actors negotiate an agreement, authorize a commitment, and produce a verifiable commit that changes the authoritative collective state.

Everything else builds upon this primitive.

The broader objective is a reusable experimental infrastructure for collective organization.

A questionnaire describes a collective.

A network reveals possible relationships.

A scenario proposes a transformation.

A commitment expresses an undertaking.

A commit changes the collective state.

And a game allows us to investigate how these transformations become possible.
